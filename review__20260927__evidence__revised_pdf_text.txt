Plain Language Summary
River-carbon models represent downstream transport, carbon inputs and exchange with the
atmosphere. A concentration measurement constrains their combined effect, but does not necessarily
separate those effects. We reanalysed 120 selected samples from the publicly archived East River
campaign of Saccardi and Winnick (2021). The measurements were collected by that study, not by the
present authors. Our calculations compare a baseline transport model with rules that supply a learned
source term or adjust the gas-exchange rate. A simplified routing tree carries the forward calculation; a
separate public hydrographic network supplies mapping and spatial-filtering information. For the main
learners, training targets were rebuilt without the concentrations in the held-out reach. The multilayer
perceptron gave a lower sample-weighted concentration error than the baseline, whereas the random
forest did not. The gain was uneven across reaches, and the calculated flux diagnostics differed greatly.
Those diagnostics overlap in spatial support and are not watershed totals or independently measured
fluxes. A separate error-profile calculation shows compensation between a source term and gas
exchange when upstream concentrations are fixed. This is a conditional explanation of the model
balance, not proof that all concentration datasets fail to distinguish processes. Unresolved spatial
support and numerical clipping still limit the empirical comparison. The case therefore motivates
checking how a model obtains a good concentration fit, alongside checking the fit itself.

Abstract
River-carbon models combine transport, unresolved sources and sinks, and air–water gas exchange.
Concentration error measures their combined prediction, not the validity of each process allocation. We
reanalysed 120 publicly archived East River observations (Saccardi and Winnick, 2021), grouped into
eight logical reaches. Forward transport uses a simplified routing tree, USGS outlet discharge and
published synoptic tributary discharge; a separate NHDPlus HR network supports mapping and
filtering. We compare a zero-residual Baseline, learned residual source–sink closures, and a
multiplicative gas-transfer correction. The main learners rebuild residual or transfer-velocity targets
within each reach-held-out fold. Sample-weighted concentration RMSE was 0.0486 mol m⁻³ for the
Baseline, 0.0372 for the multilayer perceptron (MLP), and 0.1011 for the random forest. The MLP gain
was concentrated in one three-sample reach, and three held-out MLP concentrations were clipped to
zero. Overlapping sample-area flux diagnostics were 1.21×10³, 1.94×10⁶ and 7.28×10⁵ mol d⁻¹,
respectively; they are not watershed totals. The gas-transfer correction did not lower RMSE, and 60% of
full-data inverse targets lacked a positive transfer-only solution. A conditional deterministic profile SSE,
holding diagnosed upstream concentrations fixed and sharing one transfer scale and source across
samples, gave RMSE 0.0360–0.0396 mol m⁻³ over transfer scales 0.01–1. Its single-sample zero valley is
an algebraic property, not a network identifiability proof. Resolved filter-scale results remain
sampling-conditional, while spatial-support inconsistencies and the separate sparse branch require
correction. The archived experiment illustrates why concentration skill and process-allocation
diagnostics should be reported separately; it does not establish a validated transferable closure.
Keywords: river carbon cycling; environmental model evaluation; subgrid closure; gas-transfer velocity;
grouped cross-validation; practical equifinality
1. Introduction
Rivers link terrestrial, aquatic, and atmospheric carbon cycling by transporting dissolved carbon,
transforming it through biological and biogeochemical processes, and exchanging CO₂ with the
atmosphere. Riverine CO₂ evasion is a substantial component of inland-water carbon budgets, but both
the magnitude and dominant controls of that flux vary across river networks and over time (Hotchkiss et
al., 2015; Gómez-Gener et al., 2021; Battin et al., 2023). Predictive models must therefore represent
several processes that operate simultaneously: downstream advection, lateral and internal carbon
inputs, in-stream transformation, and air-water gas exchange. At the reach scale, however, observations
and model states average over spatial heterogeneity that may occur over much shorter distances. A
reach-scale model can consequently reproduce concentration while compensating for unresolved source
or sink processes through another term in the mass balance. This possibility matters whenever model
performance is judged mainly from concentration observations.
The East River watershed provides a useful setting in which to examine this problem because a
process-based stream-network CO₂ model has already been developed for the system. Saccardi and
Winnick (2021) represented downstream transport together with CO₂ sources and atmospheric
exchange to predict spatial variation in stream CO₂ concentrations and fluxes. Their work illustrates
how several process terms jointly determine the same downstream concentration state. Gas exchange is
commonly represented through an empirical transfer velocity, and Raymond et al. (2012) related
gas-transfer velocity to hydraulic properties of streams and small rivers. Such parameterizations make
gas exchange operational at network scale, but they do not remove uncertainty in the other terms of the
carbon balance. Groundwater inputs, lateral inflows, metabolic heterogeneity, and other sub-reach
processes may remain incompletely observed or resolved. Errors in these processes and errors in gas
exchange can therefore enter the same concentration equation and potentially offset one another.
This compensation complicates model evaluation because different process allocations can produce
similar concentration responses. Bennett et al. (2013) argued that environmental-model performance
should be characterized using evidence appropriate to model purpose rather than a single
goodness-of-fit statistic. Vilas et al. (2023) likewise treated model-data discrepancy as information that
may arise from the model, the observations, or their interaction, while Markovich et al. (2022) evaluated

alternative methods explicitly in the presence of model error. For river-carbon transport, these
considerations imply that a lower concentration error is not necessarily sufficient to identify which
process representation is better supported. If an unresolved source-sink correction and the gas-transfer
velocity can both alter the same predicted concentration, optimizing either term may reduce residual
error. The relevant test is whether their effects remain distinguishable when each candidate closure is
coupled back to the transport model and evaluated under reach-grouped transport coupling, subject to
the boundary-conditioning and target-construction limitations described below.
Machine learning provides one possible representation of unresolved process terms. In climate
modeling, for example, spatial coarse-graining has been used to define unresolved tendencies and to
train data-driven subgrid parameterizations, including models tested across resolutions (Yuval &
O’Gorman, 2020). Related work on data-driven closure of river-carbon transport is also in preparation
(Gao et al., manuscript in preparation). The central difficulty is not whether a flexible model can fit a
diagnosed residual in the data used for training, but whether that residual representation remains
useful after transfer to a new spatial group and reinsertion into the governing balance. A residual may
encode repeatable unresolved dynamics, but it may also absorb errors in hydraulic representation,
boundary conditions, observations, or other model components. Strong in-sample fit therefore provides
limited evidence for a closure unless its effect is evaluated through the coupled model under held-out
conditions.
Here we examine this question with 120 public East River observations assigned to eight logical
reaches. Three parts of the analysis serve different purposes. First, predictions from residual and
gas-transfer closures are reinserted into the quasi-steady balance before held-out concentration is
scored. Second, spatial filtering on an NHDPlus HR representation describes how a diagnosed
source–sink residual, S_sgs, changes with aggregation. Third, an algebraic objective surface separates
local source–exchange compensation from the predictive comparison. A sparse representation provides
an additional descriptive experiment, but its archived evaluation does not yet meet the main protocol's
fold-local target requirement. Our contribution is this explicit separation of prediction, residual
diagnosis and conditional process allocation in a public-data case study. We do not claim that the
balance equation, empirical transfer relation, public observations or algebraic non-uniqueness were
newly discovered here. Unequal sampling, empirical geometry, unresolved spatial support and the
absence of independent evasion measurements bound the conclusions.
2. Methods
2.1 Study data and river-network representation
The study reuses public observations from the upper East River watershed near Almont, Colorado
(hydrologic unit code 14020001). The analysis selects 120 dated, model-included samples from the
archived campaign table of Saccardi and Winnick (2021), collected between 2 and 11 August 2019. This
number describes our selected analysis set, not necessarily the entire original campaign. Logical
reaches R001–R008 contain 1, 3, 15, 24, 17, 1, 1 and 58 samples, respectively (Table 1). The three
single-sample reaches enter the routing bookkeeping but provide limited evidence for transfer. These
eight labels define evaluation groups; they are not eight statistically independent replicates of a
catchment-scale experiment. The production routing tree contains seven tributary nodes and six
mainstem segments, R008_S1–S6. Mainstem order and tributary attachment follow confluence-northing
thresholds. This is a simplified logical tree, not a verified path-compressed representation of the fine
NHD directed graph.
Three public sources supply distinct inputs. The HydroShare supplement provides campaign chemistry
and synoptic tributary discharge. NHDPlus HR flowlines for HUC 14020001 provide a separate mapping
and filtering network, whose directed topology uses endpoint matching and the flow-direction attribute.
That fine network must not be substituted verbally for the logical tree used in forward transport.
Mainstem outlet discharge comes from USGS gage 09112500 on the sample dates; tributary discharges
use the published synoptic values without gage-ratio scaling. Sample identifiers join chemistry to logical
reaches, projected locations assign samples to mainstem segments, and fine-flowline mappings support

2.2 Quasi-steady CO₂ mass balance and gas exchange
Each sample is associated with a model support length L and computed width W. In the archived
production calculation, L comes from within-segment sample-midpoint spacing, with a 100 m fallback
for a single sample, rather than a bounded tessellation of a verified reach domain. The resulting support
is a current limitation, not a measured reach length. Carbon balance is evaluated quasi-steadily for each
sampling date; this is not a time-integrated daily simulation. With equal inflow and outflow discharge
within a control volume and no separate lateral term, advection, unresolved sources and atmospheric
exchange satisfy
Eq. (1): Q(C_in - C) + (A_s/tau_d)[S_sgs - k(C - C_eq)] = 0

where Q is discharge (m³ s⁻¹), C_in and C are upstream and control-volume concentrations (mol m⁻³), k
is transfer velocity (m d⁻¹), and S_sgs is the areal source–sink term (mol m⁻² d⁻¹). C_eq is
atmospheric-equilibrium concentration, A_s = L·W is water-surface planform area (m²), and tau_d =
86400 s d⁻¹ converts daily areal flux to mol s⁻¹. Planform area is distinct from hydraulic cross-section
area. The archived hydraulic inputs use the three empirical relations in Section 2.1: their product uWh
= 0.97888Q is close to, but not exactly, Q. We therefore do not claim that the stored empirical u is
exactly Q/(Wh); changing to a continuity-derived velocity would require updating k and the derived
predictors. For residual diagnosis, C is observed and S_sgs is inferred. For forward simulation, the
closure supplies S_sgs and C is solved. Dividing Eq. (1) by A_s/tau_d gives the daily areal form
Eq. (2): q_A(C_in - C) + S_sgs - k(C - C_eq) = 0
in which q_A = tau_d·Q/A_s (m d⁻¹) is a daily area-normalized discharge and every term has units mol
m⁻² d⁻¹.
All closures are inserted into this same balance: a closure configuration is defined entirely by how it
supplies S_sgs and k, and every configuration is scored after the identical transport calculation is
re-solved. Because the transport numerics are held fixed, differences among configurations arise from
their closure specification and the resulting allocation of model discrepancy.
Gas exchange is summarized by the model flux density
Eq. (3): F_CO2 = k(C - C_eq)
The flux diagnostic sums F_CO₂ times the assigned water-surface area A_s, giving units mol d⁻¹.
Samples can revisit or overlap spatial support, and the sum combines observations from different dates.
It is therefore neither a watershed flux at one time nor a time-integrated campaign emission. It
compares model allocations under the archived sample support; it is not an independently observed
evasion quantity.
The empirical transfer velocity follows the energy-dissipation parameterization of Ulseth et al. (2019),
which was the formulation used by the original East River model (Saccardi & Winnick, 2021) and which
accounts for the high turbulence of steep mountain streams. The Schmidt-600-normalized velocity
k_600 is estimated from the stream energy-dissipation rate e_D = g·S·u, and the CO₂-specific velocity is
obtained by Schmidt-number scaling:
ln k600 = 1.18 ln eD + 6.43 (eD >= 0.02 m2 s-3); eD = g S u; k_emp = k600 (Sc/600)^-0.5
We distinguish the Schmidt-600-normalized velocity k_600 from the CO₂-specific empirical velocity
k_emp. The high-energy branch (e_D ≥ 0.02 m² s⁻³) applies throughout the study corridor; the Schmidt
number Sc is evaluated at the sample water temperature. Because the choice of k_600 parameterization
affects every gas-exchange result, Raymond et al. (2012) Model-4 k_600 values are retained as a
sensitivity column in the processed observation table (median k_600 ≈ 98 m d⁻¹ versus ≈ 268 m d⁻¹ for
Ulseth); the primary evaluation below uses Ulseth throughout. The equilibrium concentration C_eq is
computed from Henry’s law with a temperature-dependent solubility constant. The constant follows the
NIST/Sander compilation for CO₂ in water, k^°_H(298.15 K) = 0.035 mol kg⁻¹ bar⁻¹ with a van ’t Hoff
temperature dependence d(ln k_H)/d(1/T) = 2400 K, so C_eq = k_H(T)·pCO₂·ρ_water with the
atmospheric pCO₂ fixed at 400 µatm. Colder water dissolves more CO₂, so C_eq now varies with each
sample’s temperature rather than being held at a single constant.
The routed headwater boundary uses atmospheric equilibrium evaluated at the mean temperature of
the supplied observation set; local exchange uses sample-specific equilibrium. During fold-local
diagnosis that supplied set excludes the held-out rows. This boundary assumption differs from a
measured upstream CO₂ concentration and should not be interpreted as one. Cross-section
visualizations are idealized trapezoids, and the vertical velocity profile is a schematic parabola rather
than an ADCP measurement. These display products do not add observational constraints to the
metrics.

For a source S and fixed local forcing, the explicit solution is C = [q_A C_in + S + k C_eq]/(q_A + k). A
negative numerator is incompatible with a nonnegative concentration in this steady balance. The
current routing wrapper catches that infeasibility and clips C to zero, which can leave a nonzero balance
residual. Three archived held-out MLP predictions are affected. We retain those archived predictions for
traceability, but do not treat clipping as a physically conservative solution or their RMSE as a
constraint-validated performance result.
2.3 Spatial filtering and diagnosis of the subgrid residual
Reach-scale transport formulations average over heterogeneity within each reach, and the unresolved
contributions appear formally as a residual source-sink term. Studying that term requires an operable
definition of the filter width Deltax rather than a qualitative notion of subgrid structure.
We perform spatial coarse-graining on the directed NHDPlus HR flowlines. Flowlines are first ordered
by their directed topology (upstream to downstream, from endpoint matching and the NHD
flow-direction attribute), then grouped into filter cells as individual flowlines, consecutive pairs, or
consecutive groups of four (native, ~2×, and ~4× merges). A whole-reach (study_reach) contraction
that preserves all cross-group fine edges produces a cyclic cell DAG and is therefore excluded from
formal filter claims (no singleton fallback). Each campaign observation is snapped to its nearest cell at
each resolved scale. The reported Deltax is the mean length of the unique spatial cells that contain
samples at that scale, giving Deltax ≈ 587 m for the native operator. Figure 1 summarizes the filtering
construction.
For each date and filter scale, the headwater boundary is atmospheric equilibrium; sampled upstream
states inform downstream diagnostic boundaries along directed paths. Excluding a sample's own
concentration from its upstream boundary prevents self-conditioning, but does not by itself prevent
dependence on another held-out sample. That separate requirement is addressed by fold-local target
reconstruction for the main learners. Filter summaries first use available cell-date diagnostics and then
aggregate by spatial cell. There are 39, 32 and 27 sampled spatial cells at the three scales, with 43, 36
and 32 sampled cell-date combinations, respectively. These are distinct supports, not statistically
independent replicates: cells share network connections and dates. Sparse synchronous coverage
means that a cell-mean summary is not a fully observed instantaneous filtered carbon budget.
At the operator level the construction parallels the coarse-graining used in large-eddy simulation and in
learned subgrid parameterization studies (Yuval & O’Gorman, 2020); the analogy is limited to spatial
filtering, and S_sgs denotes the residual of the filtered river CO₂ balance at the chosen scale. Once
resolved transport and gas exchange are recomputed on the filtered balance, the residual implied by the
observations is
Eq. (4): S_sgs = k(C - C_eq) - q_A(C_in - C)
S_sgs is a filter-induced closure residual. It can absorb measurement error, errors from the simplified
transport representation, and genuinely unresolved processes. It is not a direct measurement of a single
unresolved biogeochemical flux. Its magnitude, structure, and learnability are evaluated below as the
aggregation scale changes.

2.4 Alternative unresolved-process closures
Three closure configurations are compared. They differ only in how S_sgs and k are supplied to Eq. (1).
The Baseline retains the transport and hydraulic formulation of the other configurations while setting
S_sgs = 0 and using the energy-dissipation empirical velocity k_emp (Ulseth et al., 2019). It serves as
the zero-residual reference for comparison with the alternative closure formulations.
The Residual-AI configuration learns S_sgs from hydraulic and water-quality covariates. Two learners
are trained with a fixed seed (42): a multilayer perceptron and a random forest. The candidate predictor
pool comprises discharge, velocity, depth, width, slope, temperature, the available carbon chemistry
(dissolved organic carbon for 41 of the 120 samples), and the derived dimensionless quantities; fields
that are entirely absent from the campaign table are excluded, and missing retained predictors are
imputed with medians calculated from the training reaches of each fold. The multilayer perceptron uses
hidden layers of 64, 32, and 16 units with learning rate 0.001, early stopping, and fold-specific predictor
standardization, and its predicted residual is signed, allowing both source and sink values. The random
forest uses 200 trees with maximum depth 12 and no predictor standardization. The training target is
the dimensionally consistent residual of Eq. (4), diagnosed from the observed concentrations routed
through the directed network (Section 2.3).
The k-correction leaves S_sgs at zero and supplies k_eff = k_emp·exp(g_θ(X)). XGBoost predicts the
dimensionless log correction using 300 trees, maximum depth 6, learning rate 0.05 and seed 42. Within
each fold, the diagnostic balance gives k_need = q_A(C_in − C_obs)/(C_obs − C_eq), where a finite
positive value is required. The training response is log(k_need/k_emp); infeasible inverse targets are
excluded rather than replaced by a small positive velocity. In the full-data diagnostic, 72 of 120 targets
are infeasible (60%). This fraction describes that diagnostic set, not every training fold or the forward
model's domain. The fitted correction still predicts a positive velocity for every held-out sample. None of
the 120 archived reach-held-out predictions reaches the log lower bound g = −12. We report the
median of the paired ratios k_eff/k_emp, which is not generally the ratio of the two marginal medians.
The diagnosed residual is positive for all 120 samples (a subgrid source; Section 3), yet the Residual-AI
learners are left unbounded in prediction. The consequence of this signed, unbounded prediction is
examined under held-out evaluation in Section 3.

2.5 Leave-one-reach-out transport-coupled evaluation
The main closure comparison leaves one of the eight logical reaches out at a time. Within each fold,
predictor medians and any standardization are fitted on the training reaches; standardization applies to
the MLP, but not the tree learners. The fitted closure supplies values for the full network state required
by transport. The quasi-steady network is then re-solved, and only predictions for the held-out reach are
scored against observed C_aq. The sparse branch is a separate experiment with the limitations in
Section 2.8 and should not be assumed to satisfy this complete protocol. No inner
hyperparameter-selection loop is used, so the procedure is grouped cross-validation rather than nested
cross-validation.
For the main MLP, random-forest and k-correction branches, all concentration labels in the held-out
group are removed before training-target diagnosis. Training targets are then joined back by date,
reach and sample identifier. Missing predictors are imputed from the training fold, and the fitted
closure supplies the full forward network state; only the held-out keys enter scoring. Each key must
occur once in the pooled predictions. A held-out-label perturbation check tests whether training targets
remain unchanged. Forward headwaters use atmospheric equilibrium, not a fallback to the
concentration being scored. Predictor and discharge information remain conditioned on the observed
campaign, so this is a closure-transfer experiment rather than an ungauged-basin or future-forcing
forecast. Fixed hyperparameters avoid an inner selection step; the MLP's internal early-stopping split is
not an additional reach-grouped validation experiment.
R008 contributes 58 samples, whereas three reaches contribute one each. Pooled errors therefore
describe the selected samples, not an equally weighted population of reaches. We retain subgroup
results (Table 5), distinguish point differences from statistical superiority, and examine the influence of
R002. The date-grouped baseline and k-correction runs are time-sensitivity diagnostics, not an inner
level of the reach split. The sparse branch retains a different archived target-construction procedure,
described explicitly in Section 2.8.
2.6 Metrics and flux diagnostic
The primary metric is the held-out C_aq RMSE in mol m⁻³. The secondary diagnostic is the
area-weighted model flux Σ(F_CO₂·A_s) in mol d⁻¹, computed from Eq. (3) with the transport-predicted
concentration and the transfer velocity of each configuration (k_emp for the Baseline and Residual-AI,
k_eff for the k-correction), summed as an overlapping sample-area diagnostic (not a study-area total).
An observation-based proxy uses k_emp with observed concentrations. Differences in this area-weighted
total across closures indicate how each configuration allocates the balance between sources and gas
exchange; the totals are model diagnostics, not independently validated evasion measurements.
2.7 Practical equifinality diagnostic
To characterize compensation between source terms and gas exchange, we define the implied source
adjustment
Eq. (5): S_implied = (k_emp - k_eff)(C - C_eq)
At fixed concentration and resolved transport state, S_implied is the source-sink adjustment that makes
a model retaining k_emp locally equivalent to a model that uses k_eff and no additional source term. In
Eq. (5), C is the observed aqueous concentration at the sample, so the two process allocations are
compared at a common observed concentration rather than at their respective forward-model
concentrations. A large S_implied together with a small change in concentration error indicates that the
observations provide limited discrimination between the two allocations; we refer to this compensating
closure behaviour as practical equifinality.
We also calculate a deterministic objective without fitting a learned closure. For a single sample with
C_in, q_A and C_eq fixed, J(k,S) = [C_obs − C_model(k,S)]² is zero on S = S_diag + (C_obs − C_eq)(k −
k_emp). One local concentration equation with two freely varying parameters is therefore

underdetermined. This algebraic result does not establish non-identifiability of an entire routed network
or of a restricted closure family. For the pooled diagnostic, all 120 samples share one transfer scale α =
k/k_emp and one source S. The objective is an equally sample-weighted SSE, and J_prof(α) = min_S

The profile describes sensitivity under that parameterization. It is neither held-out prediction nor a
likelihood, and the code's 10% increase above minimum RMSE is a descriptive display threshold, not a
confidence boundary. Model-specific median parameters shown on the surface are reference summaries
of heterogeneous predictions, not operating points that reproduce each model's network RMSE. The
current loader reconstructs q_A through a residual identity because merged physical columns receive
suffixes; this agrees numerically with direct routed Q/A_s for the archived data, but should be replaced
by an explicit keyed physical-column join before release.
2.8 Sparse dimensionless closure
A final experiment asks whether the diagnosed residual has a compact dimensionless description. The
response is S* = S_sgs/(k_emp C_eq), and candidates are Fr, slope, h/W, log10(Re) and log10(Da). The
current implementation defines Da = (k_emp/86400)τ/h, with τ = L/u in seconds, so its time bases are
consistent. The older statement that Da lacked this conversion and was discarded is not applicable to
the archived results. LASSO with fixed penalty 0.05 provides sparse coefficients, following the
descriptive use of dimensionless candidates motivated by Xie et al. (2022).
Two fits must be distinguished. A full-data fit to S* supplies the displayed standardized and
original-variable equations; it is descriptive, not a held-out physical law. A separate grouped transport
experiment fits the dimensional S_sgs target and reinserts its predictions into the transport balance.
The archived sparse branch, unlike the main learners, builds residual targets and predictor medians
before splitting the data. Although its scaler and LASSO are refitted by fold, training targets can retain
routed dependence on held-out concentrations and preprocessing uses held-out predictor information.
Its reported reconstruction R² and transport RMSE are therefore provisional diagnostics, not strict
leakage-controlled validation. They are retained to document the existing experiment, pending fold-local
target reconstruction and imputation. Compactness alone supplies no evidence of predictive utility.
3. Results
3.1 Lower pooled MLP error does not establish stable closure transfer
The archived leave-one-reach-out predictions give a lower pooled point RMSE for the MLP than for the
Baseline, but a higher RMSE for the random forest (Tables 2 and 3; Figure 3). Concentration RMSE is
0.0486, 0.0372 and 0.1011 mol m⁻³, respectively. Corresponding MAE values are 0.0236, 0.0214 and
0.0512 mol m⁻³, and biases are −0.0236, −0.0055 and +0.0342 mol m⁻³. Thus the MLP reduces the
pooled negative bias, whereas the random forest has a positive bias. These are descriptive out-of-fold
metrics, not in-sample fit scores or evidence of a statistically established performance advantage. The
baseline date-grouped calculation gives the same 0.0486 mol m⁻³ RMSE because no baseline closure is
learned from a split.
Subgroup results distinguish the learners (Table 5; Figures 4 and S1). On R008, RMSE is 0.0278 mol
m⁻³ for the Baseline, 0.0248 for the MLP and 0.0239 for the random forest. Across R002–R005, it is
0.0635, 0.0470 and 0.1421 mol m⁻³, respectively. The MLP therefore has a lower pooled error in both
groups; the random forest improves on R008 but deteriorates across the multi-sample tributaries. R002
contains three strongly supersaturated samples (pCO₂ approximately 5455–6066 µatm) and contributes
93.8% of the total squared-error reduction from Baseline to MLP. Removing those three rows from the
archived predictions gives RMSE 0.0268 versus 0.0256 mol m⁻³. This is a scoring sensitivity, not a
retraining experiment. Taking the square root of mean reach-specific MSE gives 0.0932 versus 0.0640

3.6 Conditional objective surface and profile SSE
The single-sample surface reproduces the analytic zero valley of the fixed-boundary equation (Figure 5).
Its flat profile follows from allowing a separate source value at every transfer velocity; it is not an
empirical discovery about all possible concentration measurements. Pooling the 120 samples with a
common α and source gives a non-flat profile. RMSE ranges from 0.0360 to 0.0396 mol m⁻³ over α =
0.01–1, with the archived minimum near α = 0.05843 and S = 0.1277 mol m⁻² d⁻¹. This describes
limited change in the selected deterministic error metric across that interval. Its interpretation depends
on fixed observational upstream boundaries, sample weighting, shared parameters and the current
spatial support. It cannot be compared directly with held-out network RMSE as if it were another
validated predictive model.
4. Discussion
4.1 Concentration skill and physical validity answer different questions
With fold-local targets, the MLP lowers the archived sample-weighted concentration RMSE; the random
forest does not. The MLP gain is concentrated in R002 rather than shared uniformly across reaches, and
three held-out states require clipping. These observations separate numerical concentration fit from a
physically admissible and stable closure. This distinction follows the purpose-dependent evaluation
advocated by Bennett et al. (2013) and the treatment of discrepancy in Vilas et al. (2023). The subgroup
pattern is also model-specific: the MLP improves pooled errors on both R008 and R002–R005, whereas
the random forest deteriorates on the tributary group. A single statement that residual learning either
succeeds or fails would conceal that difference.
Two checks are needed before interpreting the MLP comparison more strongly. First, support lengths
and routing must correspond to a declared physical domain. In the archived length diagnostic, R002's
assigned control lengths total 837.76 m against a recorded NHD support of 170.22 m. That discrepancy
is especially consequential because R002 dominates the error reduction; neither recorded number
should be accepted as the true domain without path verification. Second, source predictions must yield
admissible states without hiding conservation errors. A feasibility-aware bound or constrained solve
should be defined from the current routed state, not selected to improve the held-out score. Reporting

17
source predictions, raw concentrations, applied corrections and balance residuals would make this
check auditable. These repairs change the computation, so their eventual metrics cannot be anticipated
by editing the current prose.
The Baseline is a reference configuration, not a demonstrated physical truth. A learned closure can
improve concentration error while assigning an unsupported process contribution; conversely, a poor
closure does not prove that unresolved sources are absent. The conditional surface in Figure 5 explains
local compensation, but does not show that concentration data can never distinguish processes.
4.2 Process allocation and practical equifinality
For 60% of full-data diagnostic samples, no positive k_need matches the observed concentration with
zero residual source under the assigned boundary and transport state. This is an inverse-target
feasibility statement. It does not make the learned forward model undefined: positive k_eff predictions
exist for every held-out sample, and none reaches the lower bound. Their failure to improve RMSE
should therefore be reported separately from target infeasibility. The balance nevertheless explains why
an added source and reduced gas exchange can offset one another at fixed concentration. That
compensation motivates a diagnostic of process allocation, rather than a causal explanation inferred
from a small change in aggregate error.
Here, practical equifinality is used narrowly for source–exchange compensation under the stated
conditional calculation. The single-sample zero valley is an analytic benchmark; the pooled profile adds
a data-dependent sensitivity under one shared parameterization. Neither is a formal
structural-identifiability analysis or an error-model-based likelihood assessment. The large differences
in learned-model flux diagnostics are also not, by themselves, evidence of equifinality. They show that
changing the closure changes the inferred process allocation. Reach weighting, the assumed common
source, and spatial-support repair may change the pooled profile; until those sensitivities are checked,
the reported RMSE interval should remain descriptive. Independent gas-transfer or source constraints
would address a different question from reducing concentration error alone.
This process-allocation ambiguity concerns the closure form; the filtering experiment addresses a
related but distinct source of variability in the diagnosed residual.
4.3 What filtering and sparse representation reveal about the residual
On resolved filter scales only, cell-mean |S_sgs| stays near 3.3–3.6 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ from native NHDPlus HR
resolution through ~4× merges. Study-reach aggregation is excluded after an unresolved contraction
cycle, so the manuscript does not claim scale-invariance through that coarser operator. Within the
resolved range the result is a sampling-conditional sensitivity under sparse synchronous coverage,
bounded by the implemented flow-directed operator (Yuval & O’Gorman, 2020, analogy limited to
spatial filtering).
The sparse description retains Froude number, slope and log10(Da), not a two-term relation. Its
archived reconstruction R² is negative, and its dimensional transport error exceeds the Baseline.
However, incomplete fold isolation prevents using these results as a definitive test of sparse closure
transfer. Repairing the target construction and imputation is necessary even for a negative result:
leakage does not become acceptable because a model performs poorly. The full-data coefficients can
still document associations in the diagnosed residual, provided that coefficient scaling, response
definition and validation status are stated explicitly.
4.4 Implications for environmental-model evaluation
This case separates three questions: whether concentrations are predicted more accurately, whether
the numerical states satisfy the specified balance, and whether the allocated processes have
independent observational support. A lower RMSE answers only the first. The comparison should
therefore retain concentration errors, feasibility and conservation checks, and process diagnostics as
separate outputs rather than combine them into a single claim of physical improvement.

The present conclusions are bounded by strongly unequal reach support, the hydraulic-geometry
representation of channel width and depth, incomplete covariates (alkalinity, nitrogen, phosphorus,
photosynthetically active radiation), and the absence of independent evasion measurements. The Water
Quality Portal merge and the StreamPULSE search returned no usable additional constraints for this
campaign. These limitations restrict inference to the East River experiment, and they also identify the
observations that would most help to discriminate closures: more balanced reach sampling,
better-resolved channel geometry, and independent constraints on gas exchange. The central
implication is that lower concentration error alone is insufficient to determine which allocation of
unresolved processes is better supported.
5. Conclusions
The archived reach-held-out calculation gives MLP concentration RMSE 0.0372 mol m⁻³, compared with
0.0486 for the Baseline and 0.1011 for the random forest. The MLP point reduction is concentrated in
R002 and includes three clipped states; it is not a validated generalization advantage. The k-correction
also rounds to 0.0486 and does not improve the point metric. Separately, 60% of the full-data inverse
targets lack a positive k-only solution. A fixed-boundary single-sample balance admits exact
source–exchange compensation, while the pooled deterministic profile describes a conditional,
parameterization-dependent sensitivity. These findings do not establish general network
non-identifiability. Filter-scale interpretation is restricted to the resolved coarsenings, and the sparse
branch remains provisional until its targets and preprocessing are made fold-local.
The analysis combines spatial filtering, transport-coupled grouped prediction and a conditional
objective-surface diagnostic without treating them as interchangeable evidence. Public measurements
provide the observations; the present code supplies the model predictions and residual calculations. The
immediate contribution is an auditable East River comparison and a clear boundary between
concentration skill and process attribution. Correcting spatial support, numerical feasibility and
sparse-fold isolation is necessary before a final empirical interpretation. No watershed evasion total,
independent flux validation, universal filter law or transfer to another basin is established by the current
results.
